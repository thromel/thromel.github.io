I returned to school this fall after three years as a software engineer at IQVIA, and the first months were hectic. Since September I started an M.Sc., joined a new lab, submitted a paper to ACL Rolling Review, and ran several research projects in parallel. Some weeks had more tasks than hours.

That pressure made me think again about work ethic. I think hours matter less than how I spend them. A hard worker spends them on one hard thing at a time and rests as deliberately as they work. Cal Newport's _Deep Work_ states this idea more clearly than anything else I have read. This post records the parts I use now, and the books behind them.

## One thing at a time

Newport defines deep work as professional activity in a state of distraction-free concentration that pushes your abilities to their limit. His case rests on attention residue, from Sophie Leroy's research. When you switch tasks, part of your attention stays on the previous task, and you do worse on the next one.

So a switch costs more than the seconds it takes to open another tab. Suppose I split a morning between a paper draft, email, and a half-written proof. I get three tasks, and each one gets a fraction of my attention. The alternative is to finish one task, or reach a clear stopping point, before I start the next.

## Close the drawer

Single-tasking only works if I can stop a task cleanly. I picture a cupboard with one drawer for each project. To move to another task, I shut one drawer. To stop work for the day, I shut them all.

Most people cannot do that on command, so a physical step helps. Before I stop, I write two lines: where I am, and what the next step is. Newport suggests a shutdown ritual at the end of the workday. You check that every open task is written down, and then you declare the day over. The ritual gives your mind permission to stop. Without it, the mind keeps circling open loops.

## Build systems so fewer decisions need you

I want to save my attention for the decisions that need it. For a graduate student, that means a reading queue instead of a daily choice about the next paper. It also means a weekly plan instead of a daily scramble, and one fixed place for notes. Each of these removes a small decision from the day.

## Plan for the setback

Deadlines rarely go as planned. Before the work starts, I write down what could go wrong and what I will do if it does. If the proof does not work by Thursday, I switch to the simpler case. If the experiment fails, I check the data pipeline first. The list takes ten minutes, and it stops a bad afternoon from becoming a lost week.

## Rest as its own block

Newport argues that deep concentration has a daily limit. He cites Anders Ericsson's research on deliberate practice, which found that experts sustain about four hours a day, and beginners less. Past that limit, extra hours mostly add fatigue.

So rest is part of the work. During downtime, my mind keeps processing problems I stopped thinking about. A firm stop in the evening means I start the next day with energy. Half-rest does not count. That is when I am technically off but still check messages. I want rest to be as complete as work. Sleep belongs in the plan too.

## What I do now

The fall is still busy, so I keep the rules simple. I work in long blocks with my phone in another room. I stop at a set time each day and write two lines to close each session. I schedule rest as a real block, with a proper break after the hard days.

## Books and papers behind this

- _Deep Work_ by Cal Newport (2016). The source for most of this post: attention residue, the shutdown ritual, and the daily limit on concentration.
- _Slow Productivity_ by Cal Newport (2024). Do fewer things, work at a natural pace, and obsess over quality.
- _A World Without Email_ by Cal Newport (2021). How constant messaging breaks attention into pieces, and how teams can replace it with clearer processes.
- _Rest_ by Alex Soojung-Kim Pang (2016). Treats deliberate rest as a skill. Many productive scientists and writers did about four hours of focused work a day.
- _Peak_ by Anders Ericsson and Robert Pool (2016). The research on deliberate practice behind the four-hour limit.
- _Getting Things Done_ by David Allen (2001). Capture every open loop in a trusted system, so your mind does not have to hold it.
- _Four Thousand Weeks_ by Oliver Burkeman (2021). You cannot do everything, so choose on purpose what you neglect.
- _Attention Span_ by Gloria Mark (2023). Research on how often people switch between screens and tasks, and what each switch costs.
- _The Art of Doing Science and Engineering_ by Richard Hamming (1997). Includes his talk "You and Your Research," about choosing important problems and working on them steadily.
- Sophie Leroy, "[Why is it so hard to do my work? The challenge of attention residue when switching between work tasks](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2009.04.002)" (2009). The original attention-residue study, in _Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes_.
